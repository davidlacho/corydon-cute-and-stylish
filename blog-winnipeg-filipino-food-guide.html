Winnipeg's Filipino food scene: lumpia, lechon, and silog breakfasts

Winnipeg Guides • 4 min read • September 7, 2026

Winnipeg has the largest Filipino population per capita of any city in Canada, and the third largest in raw numbers, at somewhere north of 50,000 people. Settlement began in 1959, when early arrivals, many of them medical professionals, started building a community here decades before large-scale Filipino immigration became common elsewhere in the country. That history shows up on the plate: this is not a handful of fusion spots borrowing Filipino flavours, but a working restaurant scene serving the community that built it, concentrated mainly in the West End and St. James.

West End: Kalan and the Arlington Street corridor

The West End, roughly the Ellice, Sargent, and Arlington Street area, has one of the city's densest concentrations of Filipino households, and the restaurants along it reflect that. Kalan, at 1449 Arlington Street, has been open since 2010 and takes its name from the traditional clay cooking pot used in Filipino kitchens. It is a straightforward, sit-down spot rather than a fast-casual counter, serving the kind of home-style dishes, adobo, sisig, pancit, that a Filipino household would actually cook rather than a tourist-facing sampler plate.

St. James: Max's and its fried chicken

Max's Restaurant, at 1255 St. James Street, is the Winnipeg outpost of a chain that started in Manila and is known across the Philippines as "the house that fried chicken built." The draw is exactly what the nickname promises: a whole fried chicken, brined and fried to a shattering crust, served family-style with rice and a vinegar-forward dipping sauce. It is one of the more recognizable Filipino brand names to land in the city, and it gives a newcomer to Filipino food an easy, unambiguous starting point.

Pampanga Restaurant & Banquet Hall

Pampanga, at 349 Henry Avenue, has been family-run since 2005 and does double duty as both a restaurant and an event hall for the community it serves. It is particularly known for silog, the category of Filipino breakfast plates built around garlic fried rice and a fried egg alongside a protein (tapa, tocino, or longganisa are common pairings), and for lumpiang Shanghai, thin, tightly rolled pork spring rolls fried until the wrapper shatters. A banquet hall on site means Pampanga also hosts the kind of large family gatherings, christenings, birthdays, reunions, that Filipino food culture is built around.

What to order if the cuisine is new to you

A handful of dishes turn up across most of these menus and are a reasonable place to start. Lumpia are thin, crisp spring rolls, usually pork-filled. Lechon is whole roast pig, prized for its crackling skin, and shows up whole at larger gatherings or by the plate at smaller ones. Sisig is a sizzling-plate dish of chopped pork, typically the head and liver, seasoned with calamansi and chili. Adobo, meat braised in vinegar, soy sauce, and garlic, is closer to a everyday staple than a special-occasion dish, and pancit covers a family of stir-fried noodle dishes that vary by region. None of these need much explanation from a server; they are comfort food in the Philippines the same way perogies or a French dip are here.

Planning a visit

These restaurants are spread across different parts of the city rather than clustered in one walkable strip, so a Filipino food outing works better by car or rideshare than on foot. Kalan sits in the West End, Max's is a short drive further west in St. James, and Pampanga is north toward the Health Sciences Centre area. Hours and menus can shift, particularly around family-run spots that also host private events, so it is worth checking each restaurant's own page or calling ahead before making a special trip.

Staying at the Corydon Airbnb: the West End and St. James sit on the opposite side of downtown from Corydon Avenue, roughly a fifteen-minute drive, so this is a trip to plan as a dedicated outing rather than something to fold into a walk from the house.
